  <span class="article-summary-text">
    <span class="article-title">Borders, Mobility and the Price of Peace in Africa</span>
    <span class="article-byline">African Border &amp; Mobility Society (ABMS) &middot; 21 October 2026</span>
  </span>
</summary>
<div class="article-body">

<article class="voice-post">

Every 21 September, the International Day of Peace asks the world to consider what allows societies to live without violence. This year's call to "invest in peace" is usually read as a call for more mediators, peacekeepers and national dialogues. These matter, but they tend to arrive after institutions have already failed, and some of the clearest early evidence of that failure appears at the border. For the African Border &amp; Mobility Society (ABMS), the question is not only whether Africa invests enough in peace, but whether it invests in the places where peace is lived, tested and sometimes denied.

Niger offers a sobering lesson. In 2015, under pressure from European partners to curb irregular migration, it criminalised the facilitation of migrant transport through Agadez. The law was meant to secure a border. Researchers documented that it instead dismantled a regional transit economy that had sustained drivers, hosts and traders, pushed journeys onto remoter and deadlier desert routes, and left many people with grievances and few lawful alternatives — thus impeding intra-Africa mobility. Niger's military authorities repealed the law in November 2023. Whatever one thinks of that government, the episode showed how a policy built around restriction can generate the insecurity it claims to prevent.

Nigeria's closure of its land borders in August 2019, maintained until December 2020, told a similar story. The aim was to curb smuggling, particularly of rice. Yet the costs fell heavily on traders, transporters and border communities whose livelihoods depended on daily crossings, while informal networks adapted faster than enforcement. A highly militarised border is not necessarily a well-governed one, and a closed border is not automatically a peaceful one.

Sudan's war, which began in April 2023, shows the other side of the argument: borders as humanitarian infrastructure. Millions of Sudanese have fled to Chad, South Sudan, Egypt and beyond. Chad, one of the world's poorest states, has received hundreds of thousands of people at crossings such as Adré, whose opening to aid convoys in 2024 kept vital supplies moving. Egypt, by contrast, tightened entry requirements for Sudanese in 2023, pushing many towards irregular routes. Whether a border admits, protects or repels someone fleeing violence determines whether displacement remains a manageable crisis or becomes a spiral of exploitation.

Mobility is also an economic question with consequences for peace. Along the Goma–Gisenyi frontier between the Democratic Republic of the Congo and Rwanda, thousands of small-scale traders, many of them women, cross daily to buy and sell. When M23 fighters seized Goma in January 2025, that border became a barometer of whether civilian livelihoods could survive armed conflict. Instruments such as the COMESA Simplified Trade Regime, which lets small traders clear low-value goods with reduced paperwork, point to what better governance can achieve: fewer bribes, fewer humiliating encounters, and stronger incentives for people to protect the stability on which their income depends.

The continental promise is real but fragile. The African Continental Free Trade Area has been operational since 2021, and the African Union adopted its Protocol on Free Movement of Persons in 2018, yet the protocol had not, at last count, gathered the ratifications needed to enter into force. In West Africa, which pioneered free movement through the 1979 ECOWAS Protocol, Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger left the bloc in January 2025 and formed their own Alliance of Sahel States. Both sides have signalled a wish to keep citizens moving, which suggests that mobility rights can survive political rupture, but only if leaders treat them as a public good rather than a bargaining chip. In South Africa, campaigns by groups such as *Operation Dudula* and co. to bar foreign nationals from clinics and eventually push them out of the country show how quickly the language of African unity collapses when migrants meet vigilantes. Integration is measured less by what is signed than by how a nurse, a trader or a border officer treats someone from the next country.

Pastoral mobility is where these pressures meet. ECOWAS adopted a transhumance regime in 1998, including a certificate for herders crossing between member states, but implementation has been uneven. As jihadist violence has spread from the Sahel towards northern Benin and Togo, analysts have warned that treating herding communities as suspected militants risks pushing them towards the very groups states fear. Climate change raises the stakes: Lake Chad has shrunk dramatically since the 1960s, and successive droughts in the Horn of Africa have pushed herders across borders in search of water. Crossing a border for water is not a security threat. The danger lies in the absence of credible systems for negotiating access and resolving disputes.

Border governance is also becoming digital, and exclusion can become automated. Kenya's Nubian, Somali and other border communities have long faced additional vetting for identity documents, and when the government introduced its Huduma Namba registration in 2019, the High Court insisted that data protection safeguards accompany it. As biometric systems, passenger databases and cross-border data-sharing expand, the lesson is that a person misrecorded by an opaque system can become permanently suspect. Digital borders should be judged by fairness, proportionality and access to remedy, not only by speed.

What should investing in peace mean in practice? ABMS urges six commitments.

1. Border governance should be treated as a civilian, developmental and peacebuilding responsibility, not only a security function, and the Niamey Convention on cross-border cooperation, in force since 2020, offers a normative foundation.
2. States should widen affordable channels for lawful mobility while sharpening intelligence-led responses to serious transnational crime.
3. Border communities, who are usually first to notice shifts in markets, water access, recruitment and household vulnerability, should be built into early-warning and policy design from the outset rather than consulted after a crisis.
4. Women, young people, migrants, refugees, pastoralists and small traders should be treated as participants in border governance, not as vulnerable categories or security concerns.
5. Investment should flow to cross-border markets, public services, dispute-resolution mechanisms and shared natural-resource management.
6. Digital border technologies should operate under transparent legal and ethical standards that protect privacy, equality and redress.

For millions of people, peace is not an abstract national condition; it is encountered in motion. It is crossing without extortion or humiliation, the confidence that displacement will not erase one's rights, and the possibility of earning a living without being treated as a criminal. Africa's borders can be sites of suspicion and division, or institutions of connection and shared security. The choice lies not in whether borders should exist but in how they are governed and whose interests that governance serves. On this International Day of Peace, ABMS calls on governments, regional institutions, researchers and practitioners to place borderlands and human mobility at the centre of Africa's peace agenda.

Peace does not end at the border. In many places, that is precisely where it must begin.

    <span class="article-title">Correcting the Map, Not Yet the Border: What Africa's True Size Means for Its Future</span>
    <span class="article-byline">Samuel K. Okunade, PhD. &middot; 14 September 2026</span>
  </span>
</summary>
<div class="article-body">

<article class="voice-post">

For 450 years, the world has viewed Africa through a distorted lens. The Mercator projection, drawn up in the sixteenth century to help European sailors navigate open water, quietly did something else along the way: it shrank Africa. On the map most of us grew up with, Greenland looks roughly the same size as the African continent. In reality, Africa is about fourteen times larger. That is not a rounding error. It is a visual inheritance of the same colonial gaze that carved up the continent at Berlin in 1884, and it has shaped how generations of students, policymakers and investors have unconsciously sized up Africa's weight in the world.

On 5 September 2026, the UN General Assembly voted 164 to one, with six abstentions, to back a resolution encouraging governments, classrooms and institutions to adopt the Equal Earth projection instead. The campaign was led by Togo, on behalf of the African Union, with backing from the Bahamas. As Togo's foreign minister put it, maps do more than describe geography; they instruct the imagination. A map that shrinks a continent, generation after generation, shrinks the world's imagination of that continent's possibilities.

It is worth being precise about what this resolution does and does not do. It does not move a single boundary post between Nigeria and Cameroon, or Kenya and Tanzania. Border communities living astride the lines drawn up in Berlin remain exactly where they were, still negotiating the everyday realities of currency differences, checkpoint bureaucracy and kin split across frontiers. This is not a redrawing of Africa's political map. It is a correction of the world's mental map.

But mental maps matter for very material reasons. A continent that appears small on the wall of every classroom, boardroom, and newsroom on earth is a continent more easily dismissed in the calculus of trade, investment, and diplomatic weight. Africa's actual landmass, some 30 million square kilometres, could contain the United States, China, India, and most of Europe within its outline, with room to spare. When that scale finally becomes visible, it becomes harder to talk about the continent as an afterthought and easier to talk about it as the geographic and demographic centre of gravity it is fast becoming, home to the youngest and fastest-growing population on earth.

For regional integration efforts such as the African Continental Free Trade Area, an accurate map is a small but symbolically weighty ally. It reinforces a claim African leaders have been making for decades: that the continent's fragmentation into fifty-four states, and its historic diminishment on the world stage, are both artefacts of decisions made elsewhere, and both are things Africans are entitled to correct. The borders on the ground remain the unfinished business of a longer conversation. But the map on the wall, at last, tells the truth about the space in which that conversation is happening.

</article>
