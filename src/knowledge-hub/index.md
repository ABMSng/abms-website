<summary>
  <span class="article-summary-text">
    <span class="article-title">Correcting the Map, Not Yet the Border: What Africa's True Size Means for Its Future</span>
    <span class="article-byline">Samuel K. Okunade, PhD. &middot; 14 September 2026</span>
  </span>
</summary>
<div class="article-body">

<article class="voice-post">

For 450 years, the world has viewed Africa through a distorted lens. The Mercator projection, drawn up in the sixteenth century to help European sailors navigate open water, quietly did something else along the way: it shrank Africa. On the map most of us grew up with, Greenland looks roughly the same size as the African continent. In reality, Africa is about fourteen times larger. That is not a rounding error. It is a visual inheritance of the same colonial gaze that carved up the continent at Berlin in 1884, and it has shaped how generations of students, policymakers and investors have unconsciously sized up Africa's weight in the world.

On 5 September 2026, the UN General Assembly voted 164 to one, with six abstentions, to back a resolution encouraging governments, classrooms and institutions to adopt the Equal Earth projection instead. The campaign was led by Togo, on behalf of the African Union, with backing from the Bahamas. As Togo's foreign minister put it, maps do more than describe geography; they instruct the imagination. A map that shrinks a continent, generation after generation, shrinks the world's imagination of that continent's possibilities.

It is worth being precise about what this resolution does and does not do. It does not move a single boundary post between Nigeria and Cameroon, or Kenya and Tanzania. Border communities living astride the lines drawn up in Berlin remain exactly where they were, still negotiating the everyday realities of currency differences, checkpoint bureaucracy and kin split across frontiers. This is not a redrawing of Africa's political map. It is a correction of the world's mental map.

But mental maps matter for very material reasons. A continent that appears small on the wall of every classroom, boardroom, and newsroom on earth is a continent more easily dismissed in the calculus of trade, investment, and diplomatic weight. Africa's actual landmass, some 30 million square kilometres, could contain the United States, China, India, and most of Europe within its outline, with room to spare. When that scale finally becomes visible, it becomes harder to talk about the continent as an afterthought and easier to talk about it as the geographic and demographic centre of gravity it is fast becoming, home to the youngest and fastest-growing population on earth.

For regional integration efforts such as the African Continental Free Trade Area, an accurate map is a small but symbolically weighty ally. It reinforces a claim African leaders have been making for decades: that the continent's fragmentation into fifty-four states, and its historic diminishment on the world stage, are both artefacts of decisions made elsewhere, and both are things Africans are entitled to correct. The borders on the ground remain the unfinished business of a longer conversation. But the map on the wall, at last, tells the truth about the space in which that conversation is happening.

</article>
